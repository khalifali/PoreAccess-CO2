\begin{frame}{}
\vfill

{\LARGE\color{tumblue}\textbf{Motivation and research question}}\par\vspace{0.5cm}
{\large Why should inlet-connected pathways matter?}
\vfill
\note{So, why should inlet pathways matter? The gas has to enter the packing through connected pore pathways, and their accessibility depends on the local particle arrangement.}

\begin{frame}{Why examine access from the inlet?}

\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.47\textwidth}\hi{What porosity misses}\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Porosity measures how much void space exists.
\item It does not describe bottlenecks, path lengths or parallel connections.
\item Similar void fractions can therefore hide different gas-supply pathways.
\end{itemize}
\column{0.49\textwidth}\hi{Why the inlet is the reference}\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Gas enters from a reservoir below the bed.
\item Adsorption removes gas as it diffuses into the packing.
\item Access from the inlet may therefore help explain transient uptake differences.
\end{itemize}
\end{columns}
\vspace{0.25cm}
\small Application: CO$_2$ adsorption in zeolite 13X, used here as a controlled model problem.
\vspace{0.2cm}
\result{Hypothesis: inlet-connected diffusive pathways contain information that porosity misses.}

\note{In other words, why should we care specifically about accessibility from the inlet? Packed-bed adsorption models typically use porosity as one of the main structural descriptors. Porosity tells us how much void space is available, but it does not tell us how this void space is connected. For example, it does not describe bottlenecks, path lengths, or the number of parallel pathways through which the gas can move. So two beds with very similar porosities can still provide quite different gas-supply pathways. In applications such as CO$_2$ adsorption, the gas enters the packed bed from a reservoir at the bottom. This makes the inlet a physically meaningful reference boundary. At the same time, adsorption continuously removes CO$_2$ from the gas phase as the gas diffuses through the packing. So the ability to supply gas from the inlet toward the interior may become important for the transient response. This motivates us to test inlet-to-interior accessibility as an additional structural descriptor. And I want to emphasize that this is a hypothesis that we are testing. We are not claiming that the inlet structure alone controls all adsorption time scales. CO$_2$ adsorption in zeolite provides us with a controlled application in which we can investigate this question.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Twenty beds isolate variability caused by random arrangement}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.43\textwidth}\small
    \begin{tabular}{ll}\toprule
    Quantity & Fixed value\\\midrule

    $D_{\rm tube}/d_p$ & 10\\

    \end{tabular}
    \column{0.53\textwidth}\small
    \hi{Controlled diffusion--adsorption problem}
    \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
    \item 20 beds generated by LAMMPS DEM: gravity pouring followed by damped settling
    \item same beads, tube and filling protocol; only the insertion seed changes
    \item dry, isothermal diffusion--adsorption; gas supplied from below, no imposed flow
    \item no heat effects or competitive water adsorption
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.20cm}
  {\scriptsize\color{tumgray}
  $c_{\rm in}=y_{\rm CO_2}p/(RT)\approx6.05$ mol m$^{-3}$ at 15 vol.\%, 1 bar and 298 K.\\
  15\% represents coal-combustion flue gas (typical CO$_2$: 10--15\%).\\
  A dry, cooled reference gas fixes the driving concentration across all beds.\\
  \href{https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2016/01/f28/QTR2015-4E-Carbon-Dioxide-Capture-Technologies.pdf}{Source: U.S. DOE, Quadrennial Technology Review (2015), Sec. 4.E.}\par}
  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \result{The ensemble tests arrangement effects under otherwise identical conditions.}
\note{We generate twenty beds using LAMMPS discrete-element simulations. The fix pour command inserts beads above the bed, and gravity fills the cylindrical tube. After insertion, damped settling brings the packing to rest. The beads, tube and filling procedure stay fixed; only the insertion seed changes. Because the bed has a free upper surface, the final bed height is not prescribed; it is an outcome of the random settling process. The cross-sectional area and total solid volume are fixed, but slightly different final bed heights give slightly different bed volumes and therefore slightly different porosities, even though every case uses the same number of monodisperse particles. The inlet value corresponds to a CO2 mole fraction of approximately 15 percent at one bar: c equals y times p divided by RT. The DOE report gives a typical coal-combustion flue-gas range of 10 to 15 percent CO2, which motivates this representative concentration. Temperature is held at 298.15 K and the gas is dry, so this is a cooled, simplified reference condition. We do not simulate hot, wet industrial flue gas or its other constituents. Keeping the same reservoir concentration for all twenty beds isolates arrangement effects. The cited concentration range motivates the boundary condition, not a claim of industrial process validation.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pore-network geometry and throat diffusion}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.46\textwidth}
      \hi{Network construction}
      \footnotesize
      \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
        \item four bead centres define a Delaunay tetrahedron; its valid circumcentre represents a pore node
        \item tetrahedra sharing a triangular face are joined by a throat
        \item topology comes from particle coordinates, not voxels
      \end{itemize}
      \vspace{0.12cm}
      \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
        \item $2^{22}$ Sobol points sample the solid and gas space.
        \item Gas-point counts allocate the calculated void volume to pores.
      \end{itemize}
    \column{0.50\textwidth}
      \hi{Diffusion through throat $i$--$j$}
      \[
        G_{ij}=D_m\frac{A_{ij}}{L_{ij}},\qquad
        \dot n_{ij}=G_{ij}(c_j-c_i).
      \]
      \footnotesize
      $D_m=1.5\times10^{-5}$ m$^2$ s$^{-1}$: molecular CO$_2$ diffusivity in the gas.
      \par\smallskip
      $L_{ij}=\lVert\mathbf{x}_j-\mathbf{x}_i\rVert$ is the pore-centre distance.
      The irregular opening between three beads is approximated by an equivalent
      circular area
      \[
        A_{ij}=\pi r_{ij}^{2},
      \]
      where $r_{ij}$ is the clearance of the shared Delaunay face.
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.16cm}
  \result{The circular throat area is a geometric modelling assumption; $G_{ij}$ has units of m$^3$ s$^{-1}$.}
\note{The particle centres define Delaunay tetrahedra. Valid circumcentres become pore nodes, and a shared triangular face defines a candidate throat. D sub m is the prescribed molecular diffusivity of CO2 in the gas, 1.5 times ten to the minus five square metres per second. It is a fixed model input, not the fitted bed-scale effective diffusivity. Conductance equals this diffusivity times the approximate opening area divided by pore-centre distance. Sobol sampling assigns gas volumes to the nodes. Detailed construction sketches remain in the optional appendix.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The pore network solves diffusion and adsorption together}

\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.56\textwidth}\small
\hi{Gas balance for each pore $i$}
\[
V_i\frac{dc_i}{dt}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal N(i)}G_{ij}(c_j-c_i)
+G_{iR}(c_R-c_i)-S_i.
\]
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item $V_i$: gas volume, $c_i$: gas concentration
\item $G_{ij}$: throat conductance [m$^3$ s$^{-1}$]
\item $G_{iR}$: connection to reservoir at $c_R$, zero away from the inlet
\item $S_i$: net gas transfer to adjacent beads [mol s$^{-1}$]
\end{itemize}
\column{0.40\textwidth}\small
\hi{Uptake for each bead $k$}
\[
\frac{dq_k}{dt}=k_{\rm LDF}(q_k^*-q_k).
\]
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item $q_k$: current loading [mol kg$^{-1}$]
\item $k_{\rm LDF}$ [s$^{-1}$]: linear-driving-force rate coefficient
\item $1/k_{\rm LDF}$: relaxation time at fixed gas concentration
\end{itemize}
\end{columns}
\vspace{0.15cm}
\result{One coupled system: one concentration per pore and one loading per bead.}

\note{This is the transient diffusion equation on the network, expressed as a balance on each pore volume. The first term exchanges gas with neighbouring pores, the second connects inlet pores to the finite-conductance reservoir, and the final term transfers gas to beads. Each bead has one loading equation even if several pores surround it. LDF means linear driving force: the rate is proportional to the difference between equilibrium and current loading. All pore concentrations and bead loadings are integrated together from the prescribed initial state as one coupled transient system. Numerically, the system is solved with an implicit BDF time integrator. This is suitable for a stiff diffusion--adsorption system, where fast and slow time scales coexist. The solver uses adaptive time stepping and changes the step size automatically to satisfy the prescribed relative and absolute error tolerances. The total transfer satisfies $\sum_i S_i=\sum_k m_k\,dq_k/dt$, so adsorption transfers gas into the solid inventory. In the code, positive uptake is allocated among incident pores using incidence weights multiplied by positive gas concentration, preventing empty pores from supplying gas. Desorption uses normalized incidence weights. No independent gas-flow or pressure equation is solved.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Local equilibrium sets the adsorption target}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.48\textwidth}\hi{Local equilibrium loading}\small
\[
p_k=c_kRT,\qquad q_k^*=\frac{q_s b p_k}{1+b p_k}.
\]
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{3pt}
\item $c_k$: average gas concentration around bead $k$
\item $p_k$: local CO$_2$ partial pressure [Pa]
\item $q_k^*$: equilibrium target [mol kg$^{-1}$]
\end{itemize}
\column{0.48\textwidth}\hi{Fixed material parameters}\small
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{3pt}
\item $q_s$: maximum CO$_2$ loading [mol kg$^{-1}$]
\item $b$: Langmuir affinity [Pa$^{-1}$], so $b p_k$ is dimensionless
\item $k_{\rm LDF}$: kinetic coefficient [s$^{-1}$]
\end{itemize}
\medskip
\hi{Actual loading evolves in time}
\[
\frac{dq_k}{dt}=k_{\rm LDF}(q_k^*-q_k).
\]
\end{columns}
\vspace{0.25cm}
\result{Local gas concentrations set the target. Kinetics determine how quickly beads approach it.}
\note{The local gas concentration around bead k is a normalized, pore-volume-weighted average over its associated pores. The implementation uses nonnegative concentrations in this calculation. The ideal-gas relation gives the local CO2 partial pressure and the Langmuir isotherm supplies the equilibrium loading. This equilibrium target is not a new differential unknown. The actual loading evolves through the LDF equation introduced on the preceding slide. The parameter $q_s$ is the saturation, or maximum, CO2 loading of the zeolite according to the Langmuir model; in this work we use $q_s=5.332$ mol kg$^{-1}$. It is the asymptotic loading approached at sufficiently high CO2 partial pressure, not the actual loading reached at every operating condition. The saturation loading and affinity are prescribed material parameters, and $k_{\rm LDF}$ is fixed across packings. Adsorption removes gas from the incident pores and adds it to the bead, preserving the combined inventory apart from reservoir exchange.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The pore network delivers the reference uptake curve}

\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.47\textwidth}\hi{Resolved transient outputs}\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Gas concentration $c_i(t)$ in every pore
\item Adsorbed loading $q_k(t)$ of every bead
\item Different bead loadings reveal incomplete utilization
\end{itemize}
\medskip
The detailed model retains the actual pore connections and bead associations.
\column{0.49\textwidth}\hi{Bed-average uptake}\small
\[
\overline q_{\rm PNM}(t)=\frac{\sum_k m_k q_k(t)}{\sum_k m_k}.
\]
For identical beads:
\[
\overline q_{\rm PNM}(t)=\frac{1}{N_b}\sum_{k=1}^{N_b}q_k(t).
\]
This curve is the reference for fitting the reduced 1D model.
\end{columns}
\vspace{0.25cm}
\result{Next: can a homogeneous 1D bed reproduce this complete uptake curve?}

\note{The network calculation produces many concentration and loading histories. For model reduction, we summarize the adsorbed inventory as the mass-weighted mean loading of all beads. Because the beads have equal masses, this is their arithmetic mean. We retain the full time series, not only the final loading. The PNM curve is a numerical reference under our modelling assumptions, not an experimental measurement. We now ask whether a much smaller homogeneous model can reproduce this curve.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reduce the detailed network to a homogeneous 1D bed}

\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.47\textwidth}\hi{Detailed pore-network model}\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Actual pores and throats from each packing
\item One gas concentration per pore
\item One loading per bead
\item Reference mean uptake $\overline q_{\rm PNM}(t)$
\end{itemize}
\column{0.49\textwidth}\hi{Homogeneous 1D model}\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Replace the network by 100 equal axial slices
\item Each slice spans the full tube cross-section
\item One gas concentration and one mean solid loading per slice
\item Represent unresolved pathways with a single diffusivity $D$
\end{itemize}
\end{columns}
\vspace{0.25cm}
\result{Fit $D$ so the reduced model reproduces the network's mean uptake history.}

\note{The one-dimensional cells do not come from the pore-network extraction. We create a separate grid by dividing bed height into one hundred equal slices spanning the tube cross-section. Each slice contains homogenized gas and adsorbent phases. The detailed pore paths disappear from this representation. Their impact on transient uptake is represented by one fitted diffusivity. The reduced system has two hundred concentration and loading states, while the PNM has a state for every pore and bead. We compare their mean uptake curves rather than attempting a one-to-one mapping of their internal fields.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The homogeneous 1D uptake model}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.54\textwidth}
\small
\hi{Gas balance in each axial cell}
\[
\varepsilon_b\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}
=D\frac{\partial^2c}{\partial z^2}
-(1-\varepsilon_b)\rho_p\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}.
\]
\hi{Adsorbent loading in each axial cell}
\[
\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}
=k_{\rm LDF}\left[\frac{q_s bRTc}{1+bRTc}-q\right].
\]

\vspace*{-0.2cm}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item Divide the bed height into 100 equal slices, each spanning the tube cross-section.
\item Solve gas concentration and mean solid loading together in each slice.
\item Trial $D$ changes $c$, which changes adsorption.
\end{itemize}
\column{0.43\textwidth}
\small
\hi{Average the calculated loading}
\[
\overline q_{\rm 1D}(t;D)=\frac{1}{H}\int_0^H q(z,t;D)\,dz.
\]
For 100 equal axial cells:
\[
\overline q_{\rm 1D}(t;D)=\frac{1}{100}\sum_{\ell=1}^{100}q_\ell(t;D).
\]

\vspace*{-0.3cm}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item Match bed height, porosity and inlet condition to the network.
\item Keep isotherm, kinetics and initial state fixed. Only $D$ changes.
\end{itemize}
\end{columns}
\vspace{0.1cm}
\result{$\varepsilon_b$: bed porosity, uniform in all 100 cells of each bed.}

\note{This slide describes the homogeneous one-dimensional model, not the individual pore network. Each axial cell contains gas and a homogenized amount of adsorbent. Epsilon b is the dimensionless bed void fraction, not bulk density. It is uniform over all 100 cells and constant in time, but differs slightly between packings. The mass of adsorbent per bed volume is one minus epsilon b, multiplied by the apparent bead density rho p. We solve one gas concentration and one solid loading per cell, giving 200 coupled state equations for 100 cells. The loading equation explicitly substitutes the Langmuir equilibrium relation and p equals cRT. The diffusivity appears in the gas balance. Changing it changes the gas concentration history and therefore the adsorption history. The right-hand side is a spatial average of the calculated loadings, not an additional independent evolution equation. For equal cells and uniform adsorbent density, the arithmetic average is the bed mean loading.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fit the effective diffusivity to the complete uptake curve}

\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.53\textwidth}\hi{Actual optimization trials: bed 18427}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_optimization_trials.pdf}
{\scriptsize\color{tumgray}Selected evaluations in recorded order; individual trials need not improve the fit.\par}
\column{0.44\textwidth}\hi{Curve error and fitted coefficient}\small
\[
e_m(D)=\overline q_{\rm 1D}(t_m;D)-\overline q_{\rm PNM}(t_m).
\]
\[
\mathrm{NRMSE}(D)=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_m w_m e_m(D)^2}}{\overline q_{\rm PNM}(t_f)},
\] \[ \sum_m w_m=1.
\]
\[
D_{\rm eff}=\arg\min_D\mathrm{NRMSE}(D).
\]
{\footnotesize Time weights account for unequal output spacing.}\par\smallskip
{\footnotesize Change $D$, rerun the 1D model, compare the curve and repeat.}
\end{columns}
\vspace{0.2cm}
\result{Fit one constant $D_{\rm eff}$ per bed to the complete PNM uptake curve.}

\note{The plot shows the PNM curve in black and actual recorded trials 1, 2, 4 and 8 for bed 18427. The first trial underpredicts uptake, with 38.6 percent error. Trial 2 is already close, but the optimizer explores another value at trial 4 before converging. Trial 8 is the best fit, with D equal to 8.742 times 10 to the minus 7 square metres per second and 1.41 percent error. Trial numbers denote objective evaluations, not time steps. Each curve uses one constant D throughout the simulation. For each bed, we repeatedly solve the homogeneous model with different diffusivities. The search range is $10^{-8}$ to $1.5\times10^{-5}$ m$^2$ s$^{-1}$. The upper bound is oriented by the molecular diffusivity of CO$_2$ in the gas, while the lower bound extends the search by a little more than three orders of magnitude to allow for a strong reduction of effective transport by tortuosity and constrictions. NRMSE means normalized root-mean-square error. Here we weight squared errors by elapsed time using trapezoidal weights, take the square root and divide by the final reference loading. This prevents densely saved times from dominating the fit. These comparison times are deliberately saved on a logarithmic grid, so early transients are sampled more densely. This output-time grid is separate from the BDF solver's internal adaptive time steps, which are chosen from the integration-error estimate. The $w_m$ values are therefore trapezoidal time weights used only in the fitting metric, so the objective represents elapsed physical time rather than the number of saved points. The bounded scalar optimizer operates in log10 D; it starts deterministically and previous errors guide later trials. Only D changes. This produces one reduction target per packing. The next question is whether a cheaper calculation from geometry can predict that target.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Can inlet conductance predict the fitted diffusivity?}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.47\textwidth}\hi{Calculate steady inlet access}\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Use the pore network with adsorption disabled.
\item Fix concentrations at the inlet and an interior plane.
\item Solve steady diffusion and sum the molar flux $\dot n$.
\end{itemize}
\[G_{\rm access}=\frac{\dot n}{\Delta c}\qquad[\mathrm{m^3/s}].\]
Conductance measures how much gas the inlet-connected pathways can supply per concentration difference.
\column{0.49\textwidth}\hi{Calibrate the transport coefficient}\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Pair each calculated $G_{\rm access}$ with its fitted $D_{\rm eff}$.
\item Fit $D_{\rm eff}=f(G_{\rm access})$ on training beds.
\item Predict the omitted bed's diffusivity.
\item Run its 1D model and compare the complete uptake curve.
\end{itemize}
\end{columns}
\vspace{0.2cm}
\result{One steady conductance calculation supplies the input for transient prediction.}
\note{G access is calculated from a steady diffusion solve on the network, not predicted. Total molar flux divided by the imposed concentration difference has units of cubic metres per second. We use this conductance directly as the descriptor. A relationship learned from other beds maps it to the effective diffusivity in the transient 1D model. Conductance can also correlate with final uptake, but here its target is the coefficient fitted to the complete uptake history. For validation we first predict the omitted bed diffusivity and then run its 1D model without fitting to that bed history. The optional normalization appendix presents the alternative descriptor.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the present evidence establishes}
  \begin{FlexRow}{0.58\textheight}
    \Col{0.47}{
      \hi{Supported}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item porosity alone misses important packing variability
        \item inlet-connected topology controls transient utilization
        \item one $D_{\rm eff}$ reproduces each detailed curve accurately
        \item $G_{\rm access}$ provides an interpretable predictive closure
      \end{itemize}}
    \Col{0.47}{
      \hi{Not yet claimed}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item industrial breakthrough performance
        \item effects of flow, heat or water competition
        \item transfer to other bead sizes or $D_{\rm tube}/d_p$
        \item a universal conductance--diffusivity relation
      \end{itemize}}
  \end{FlexRow}

  \vspace*{0.5cm}
  \result{The contribution is a structure-to-model closure for a controlled diffusion-driven adsorption problem.}
\note{The evidence supports a steady inlet-conductance descriptor that captures transport differences missed by bulk porosity. A single fitted effective diffusivity reproduces each transient PNM curve with a mean error of 1.58 percent. Using conductance to predict that diffusivity gives a mean held-out curve error of 2.61 percent with the power law, essentially comparable to 2.64 percent for a linear model. The result is specific to this controlled diffusion-adsorption ensemble. We do not claim industrial breakthrough performance, robustness to heat or water competition, or transfer across bead sizes, tube geometries, sorbents and operating conditions. A more general study would be needed to establish those claims.}
\end{frame}
