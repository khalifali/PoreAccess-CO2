\begin{frame}{Why examine access from the inlet?}

\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.47\textwidth}\hi{What porosity misses}\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Porosity measures how much void space exists.
\item It does not describe bottlenecks, path lengths or parallel connections.
\item Similar void fractions can therefore hide different gas-supply pathways.
\end{itemize}
\column{0.49\textwidth}\hi{Why the inlet is the reference}\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Gas enters from a reservoir below the bed.
\item Adsorption removes gas as it diffuses into the packing.
\item Access from the inlet may therefore help explain transient uptake differences.
\end{itemize}
\end{columns}
\vspace{0.25cm}
\small Application: CO$_2$ adsorption in zeolite 13X, used here as a controlled model problem.
\vspace{0.2cm}
\result{Hypothesis: inlet-connected diffusive pathways contain information that porosity misses.}

\end{frame}

\par\smallskip
{\small Identical beads:}
\[
\overline q_{\rm PNM}(t)=\frac{1}{N_b}\sum_{k=1}^{N_b}q_k(t).
\]
\column{0.51\textwidth}\small
\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{5pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{7pt}
\hi{For each pore:}
\[
\begin{aligned}
V_i\frac{d\textcolor{tumblue}{c_i}}{dt}
&=\sum_{j\in\mathcal N(i)}G_{ij}(\textcolor{tumblue}{c_j}-\textcolor{tumblue}{c_i})\\
&\quad+G_{iR}(\textcolor{tumblue}{c_R}-\textcolor{tumblue}{c_i})\\
&\quad-m_b\sum_{k\in\mathcal B(i)}w_{ik}\:\textcolor{tumred}{\frac{dq_k}{dt}}.
\end{aligned}
\]
\smallskip
\hi{For each bead:}
\[
\textcolor{tumred}{\frac{dq_k}{dt}}=k_{\rm LDF}(\textcolor{tumblue}{q_k^*}-q_k).
\]
{\footnotesize $\textcolor{tumblue}{q_k^*}$: equilibrium target from the mean gas concentration around bead $k$ and the saturation loading $q_s$ (Langmuir isotherm).\par}
\end{columns}
\smallskip
\result{One coupled system: one concentration per pore, one loading per bead.}
\note{The PNM is the structure-resolved reference for each bed, with one gas concentration per pore and one loading per bead. Averaging the identical beads' loadings gives the full reference curve $\overline q_{\rm PNM}(t)$. Blue concentrations determine the blue Langmuir equilibrium target; the red uptake rate feeds back into the gas balance. Here $m_b$ is the common bead mass and $\mathcal B(i)$ contains beads associated with pore $i$. The weights $w_{ik}$ allocate bead $k$'s uptake among its surrounding pores, with $\sum_i w_{ik}=1$ for each bead. This prevents counting its uptake more than once. During adsorption, the implementation multiplies incidence weights by positive pore concentration and normalizes per bead; during desorption it uses normalized incidence weights. Thus the allocation can depend on the state, while $q_k$ remains one loading per bead. The Langmuir target uses the mean surrounding concentration, saturation loading, and fixed affinity and temperature. The drawing is schematic; detailed equations remain in the appendix.}
\end{frame}

\note{We replace the detailed network by a homogeneous bed with 100 equal finite-volume cells, each spanning the full tube cross-section. The thin band illustrates a differential control volume; the displayed continuum balances are discretized on those cells to solve one gas concentration and one loading per cell. Equal cell volumes and uniform adsorbent density make the bed-average loading the arithmetic mean shown here. We keep the bed geometry, porosity, inlet condition, isotherm and kinetics fixed and adjust the single effective diffusivity to reproduce the full PNM uptake history for that bed. The orange $D_{
m eff}$ in the gas balance is the coefficient adjusted to reproduce the PNM reference curve. The diffusion--adsorption coupling is the same as already explained for the PNM. The curves illustrate the fitting target, not measured data or a sequence of optimization trials. The detailed model slides remain in the 1D appendix.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fit effective diffusivity to the uptake curve}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{0.54\textwidth}\hi{Trial curves: bed 18427}\par\smallskip
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_optimization_trials.pdf}
{\scriptsize\color{tumgray}Selected trials; each curve uses one constant diffusivity.\par}
\column{0.43\textwidth}\hi{Compare with the PNM reference}\small
\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{9pt}\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{9pt}
\[
e_m(D)=\overline q_{\rm 1D}(t_m;D)-\overline q_{\rm PNM}(t_m)
\]
\[
\mathrm{NRMSE}(D)
=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_m w_m\:e_m(D)^2}}{\overline q_{\rm PNM}(t_f)}
\]
{\footnotesize $w_m$: normalized weights accounting for unequal spacing of output times.\par}
\medskip
\[
D_{\rm eff}=\arg\min_D\mathrm{NRMSE}(D)
\]
\end{columns}
\medskip
{\small\color{tumdark}\centering Trial $D$ $\rightarrow$ one transient 1D solve $\rightarrow$ average cell loadings $\rightarrow$ compare\par}
\note{For each bed, we search for one effective diffusivity between $10^{-8}$ and $1.5\times10^{-5}$ m$^2$ s$^{-1}$. The upper bound is the molecular CO$_2$ diffusivity in the gas, representing diffusion without the bed's geometric restrictions. The lower bound is just over three orders of magnitude smaller. The optimizer chooses trial values adaptively in log diffusivity. For each trial, we run one transient 1D solve, obtain the loading in every finite-volume cell, and average the 100 cell loadings to form the curve shown here. We compare that history with the PNM reference using the normalized root-mean-square error, NRMSE. Output times are more densely spaced near the beginning to capture the steep initial transient. The normalized trapezoidal weights $w_m$ account for unequal time spacing, so those numerous early points do not dominate simply because there are more of them. The error is normalized by the final PNM loading. We retain the diffusivity with the smallest error. The graph shows selected recorded trials, which need not improve at every evaluation.}
\end{frame}

\note{This is a steady geometric diagnostic on the existing pore network. Adsorption and accumulation are disabled, and there is no imposed gas flow. We fix inlet pores at a high concentration and pores at or beyond the chosen interior plane at a low concentration. The implementation uses values of one and zero, so the imposed concentration difference is one. The plane is five bead diameters above the lowest retained pore. Throat conductances use the prescribed molecular gas diffusivity. A sparse linear solve balances diffusive fluxes at every free pore; only components connecting source and sink contribute. We sum the net inlet molar flux and divide by the imposed concentration difference to obtain $G_{\rm access}$ in cubic metres per second. These fixed pore concentrations are diagnostic boundary conditions, separate from the finite-transfer reservoir used in transient adsorption. During development we pair this conductance with fitted diffusivities and calibrate their relation on training beds.
